A village health worker speaks the visit. The record fills itself.

India's 10.29 lakh village health workers (ASHAs) write every home visit by hand, often twice. With Ghar Bhet ("home visit") she speaks the visit in Hindi, Marathi or English: the visit's key fields fill in, and government rules say out loud when the mother or baby must go to hospital.

The problem is real, and it happens every day

An ASHA is the health system's first contact for every pregnancy and newborn in her village. After each home visit she fills a register, a diary and, more and more, government apps.

  • 10.29 lakh ASHAs serve India's villages (PIB, January 2025). About 70,000 work in Maharashtra.
  • Over 10 crore newborn visits a year to record: 2.54 crore births (2022) and at least 4 home visits per baby under India's 2026 newborn guidelines (SSBSK), 9 for a baby at risk. Pregnancy visits come on top.
  • Records are kept twice. ASHAs in Maharashtra describe entering the same visit on paper and again in apps, late into the night (New Lines Magazine). In Pune district, two-thirds felt tired and rushed (Kawade et al., 2021).
  • Danger signs get missed. Watched on 45 home newborn visits, ASHAs checked for lethargy in only 33% and jaundice in 24%; they found it hard to ask, examine and write at the same time (Das et al., Indian Pediatrics, 2014).
  • Typing is a barrier. In a 2024 pilot, ASHAs found typing Hindi hard and used voice dictation and voice notes instead (ASHABot, arXiv 2409.10913).

Why voice makes it better

The organisers asked: "Is the problem real and frequent, and does voice make it better?" For an ASHA, voice isn't a feature on top. It is how she already reports to the nurse (the ANM, auxiliary nurse-midwife).

Her hands stay on the baby

She is holding a baby, a thermometer or a weighing sling. Speaking keeps her eyes and hands on the baby while the record fills.

She speaks, she doesn't type

She says numbers as words: "dedh sau" (150), "saadhe aath" (8.5), "ek kilo saat sau gram" (1700 g). Ghar Bhet reads all of these.

Danger is said out loud

When a rule fires, the phone says it in her language: "Refer today." She hears it in the home, not later at her desk.

How one visit works

One narrow flow, end to end, done by speaking: a home visit becomes a checked record and, if needed, a referral the nurse sees at once.

  1. She speaks the visit

    "Sunita ka baby, saat din ka, wajan ek kilo saat sau gram, doodh kam pee raha, pasli chal rahi hai." (Sunita's baby, 7 days old, 1.7 kg, feeding less, chest pulling in.)

  2. The record fills itself

    Baby 7 days old, 1700 g, feeding poorly, chest indrawing. Every value is marked in the words it came from.

  3. Fixed rules check it

    Weight under 1800 g and chest indrawing are danger signs, and with feeding less they are signs of infection (sepsis). The phone says: take the baby to the newborn unit now, call 108.

  4. It asks what's missing

    "What is the baby's temperature?" She answers by voice, says "nahi" to a yes/no question, or corrects herself: "nahi nahi, 37.8".

  5. The nurse sees it live

    She says "save karo". The nurse's board shows the open referral and a printable slip, and the nurse can ask the board out loud who was referred.

Danger signs come from fixed, cited rules

A model never decides who is in danger. Each flag comes from a rule in the government guidelines and names the document it comes from, so the nurse can check every decision. Anything she says that no rule covers is still shown to the nurse.

Measured on 60 visit transcripts it had never seen, misses included

We wrote the rules and the record format first. The test visits and their right answers were then written from that spec alone, by a separate AI writing pass that never saw the code, the way ASHAs speak (written, not recorded). We tuned on 200 of them and kept 60 sealed until the end.

  • First run: 1 danger visit missed (a Marathi way of saying "the baby moves only when touched") and 1 false alarm ("five pads… no, four").
  • After fixing both: 99.3% of fields, 38 of 38 danger visits. These came after we saw the misses, so the first run is the one to trust.
  • Today: the rule table has grown from the first 37 rules to 75. The larger table turns 7 of the 22 visits that were normal under the old rules into referrals, so the same 60 now split 45 danger and 15 normal: 45 of 45 caught, 0 false alarms.
  • A harder, independent test: 100 tricky visits written by a separate agent that never saw the code (colloquial danger phrases, other people's symptoms, denials, exact thresholds). First run: 88.3% of fields and 50 of 59 danger visits caught, 7 of 41 false alarms. Every miss is published.
  • Checked against the guidelines, not our own rules: Short cases labelled "should refer" straight from the guideline text, before the rules were run. The first run, with the 37-rule table, caught only 11 of 24. That gap is why the table grew; with 40 cases it now catches 33 of 33 that should refer, and 18 of 18 urgent cases get an urgent flag (three labels were corrected from the guideline text, and the cases are tuned on since). The nurse sees every referral as it happens

The nurse's board shows open referrals, who is due a home visit today on the 2026 schedule (days 3, 7, 14 and 42, or nine visits for a low-weight baby), and the whole village register. She can ask it out loud: "Aaj kaun kaun refer hua?" (Who was referred today?)

What it depends on, and what happens if that stops

The only outside service is the browser's free speech recognition (in Chrome, Google's online speech service). Reading the visit and the danger rules are our own plain JavaScript, with no AI service and no npm packages.

What it is not, yet

Honest limits, so nobody has to guess.

  • Not a medical device. The rules support the nurse's and doctor's judgement; they never replace it. A PHC medical officer reviews the rule table before any pilot.
  • Not yet tested with ASHAs in the field. The main test sentences were written in the style ASHAs speak, not recorded from ASHAs. The next step is a pilot with ASHAs in one Pune block.
  • Speech-to-text depends on the browser. Chrome and Edge listen in Hindi, Marathi and English and need a connection; other browsers can type.
  • The village on the board is a sample. The people are made up; visits you speak are added live. The demo board is open so anyone can try it; a pilot adds a nurse login.
